# Romeo and Juliet — William Shakespeare

## Summary
Romeo and Juliet, William Shakespeare's tragedy likely written around 1595, dramatizes the doomed love between two teenagers from the feuding households of Montague and Capulet in Verona. Romeo Montague and Juliet Capulet fall instantly in love at a masked ball and secretly marry the next day with the help of Friar Laurence, who hopes their union might end the families' ancient hatred. Street violence intervenes when Juliet's cousin Tybalt kills Romeo's friend Mercutio, prompting Romeo to kill Tybalt in revenge and be banished from Verona. As Juliet's parents force her toward a marriage with Count Paris, Friar Laurence devises a plan involving a sleeping potion to fake her death, but a failed message leaves Romeo believing Juliet truly dead. He kills himself with poison at her tomb; Juliet wakes to find him dead and stabs herself with his dagger. The lovers' deaths finally reconcile the warring families, and the play remains the archetypal Western story of young, fated, all-consuming love.

## Plot
In Verona, the ancient feud between the Montague and Capulet families erupts into a street brawl broken up by the Prince, who threatens death to any further disturbance. Romeo Montague, nursing heartbreak over another woman, sneaks into a Capulet masked ball and instantly falls in love with Juliet Capulet, who returns his feelings; the two exchange vows at her balcony that same night. With the help of Friar Laurence, who hopes their marriage might reconcile the feuding houses, and Juliet's Nurse as go-between, the pair marry secretly the next day. That same day, Juliet's fiery cousin Tybalt kills Romeo's close friend Mercutio in a street duel, and Romeo, avenging him, kills Tybalt and is banished from Verona by the Prince. Romeo secretly consummates his marriage with Juliet before fleeing to Mantua. Juliet's father, unaware of her marriage, arranges for her to wed Count Paris, and in desperation Juliet turns to Friar Laurence, who gives her a potion to feign death and be laid in the family tomb, planning to reunite her with Romeo once he is informed of the ruse. The message explaining the plan fails to reach Romeo in time, and he hears only that Juliet is dead. Grief-stricken, he buys poison, returns to Verona, kills Paris at Juliet's tomb, and drinks the poison beside her body. Juliet wakes moments later, finds Romeo dead, and stabs herself with his dagger. The grieving Montagues and Capulets, confronted with their children's deaths, finally reconcile and end their feud.

## Characters
- **Romeo Montague** — a passionate young Montague whose sudden, all-consuming love for Juliet drives the tragedy
- **Juliet Capulet** — a Capulet daughter whose love for Romeo defies her family's feud and her arranged marriage to Paris
- **Friar Laurence** — the well-meaning friar who secretly marries the lovers and devises the failed potion plan
- **The Nurse** — Juliet's loyal, earthy confidante and go-between with Romeo
- **Mercutio** — Romeo's witty friend, whose death at Tybalt's hand triggers the tragedy's fatal turn
- **Tybalt** — Juliet's hot-tempered cousin, killed by Romeo in revenge for Mercutio's death
- **Count Paris** — the nobleman Juliet's parents intend her to marry, killed by Romeo at the tomb
- **Lord and Lady Capulet** — Juliet's parents, whose insistence on her marriage to Paris precipitates the final tragedy

## Themes
- **Fated, all-consuming love** — the "star-crossed lovers" motif frames Romeo and Juliet's passion as both beautiful and doomed from the start
- **Feud and inherited hatred** — the Montague-Capulet conflict claims the young lovers as its ultimate casualties
- **Haste and impulsiveness** — the lovers' rapid courtship, marriage, and deaths all turn on decisions made in haste
- **Fate versus free will** — the play repeatedly gestures toward destiny while showing how chance and choice intertwine in the tragedy
- **Reconciliation through loss** — only the lovers' deaths finally end the families' long-standing feud

## Key Takeaways
- The balcony scene, with Juliet's "wherefore art thou Romeo," is one of the most quoted passages in English drama
- The play's prologue explicitly labels Romeo and Juliet "star-crossed lovers," foretelling the tragic ending from the outset
- Mercutio's dying curse, "a plague o' both your houses," names the feud as the true villain of the play
- Friar Laurence's sleeping-potion plan fails only because his message to Romeo is delayed, a single accident driving the tragedy
- Romeo and Juliet's secret marriage, performed within a day of meeting, exemplifies the play's theme of headlong haste
- Both lovers die by their own hand at the Capulet tomb, Romeo by poison and Juliet by his dagger
- The families' reconciliation over their children's bodies gives the tragedy its final, bitterly ironic resolution

# The War of the Worlds — H. G. Wells

## Summary
The War of the Worlds (1897) is narrated by an unnamed writer living in Woking, Surrey, when Martians land nearby in metal cylinders and begin a methodical invasion of England using towering tripod war-machines armed with a Heat-Ray and asphyxiating Black Smoke. Unlike Wells's earlier The Time Machine, which imagines humanity's own distant decline, this novel stages a present-day invasion by a technologically superior outside force, inverting the era's colonial narratives by making the British Empire itself the conquered. The narrator's ordeal, trapped near a Martian encampment and separated from his wife, runs alongside his brother's account of the panicked evacuation of London. The Martians' unstoppable advance is undone not by human weapons but by Earth's bacteria, to which they have no immunity, a resolution that has made the novel a lasting meditation on hubris, vulnerability, and the limits of technological power.

## Plot
Martian cylinders land on Horsell Common near Woking; the creatures inside assemble giant tripod fighting-machines and quickly destroy the army units sent against them using a Heat-Ray. As the Martians advance, deploying poisonous Black Smoke, mass panic drives Londoners into flight. The narrator, separated from his wife during the evacuation, is trapped for days with an unstable curate in a half-ruined house next to a Martian encampment, where he observes the Martians up close, including their practice of feeding on human blood and the spread of the alien red weed across the land. He is eventually forced to silence the curate to avoid discovery and escapes when the Martians move on. In a separate strand, the narrator's brother describes London's chaotic exodus and joins the Elphinstone sisters fleeing toward the coast, witnessing the ironclad Thunder Child ram two tripods before being destroyed itself. Returning to a seemingly abandoned London, the narrator finds the Martians already dead in their machines, killed by Earth's bacteria and pathogens against which they had no resistance. He is reunited with his wife, and the narrative closes on humanity's shaken, uncertain sense of security.

## Characters
- **The narrator (unnamed)** — a philosophical writer in Woking who witnesses the invasion firsthand and is separated from his wife
- **The narrator's brother** — follows a separate strand of the story, escaping the fall of London
- **The curate** — the narrator's unstable companion while trapped near the Martian pit; his panic endangers them both
- **The artilleryman** — a soldier the narrator meets who proposes grand but ultimately delusional plans for underground human resistance
- **The Martians** — invading aliens equipped with tripod war-machines and advanced weaponry, defeated by Earth's microbes

## Themes
- **Imperialism inverted** — England, a colonizing power, is itself colonized, forcing readers to see conquest from the conquered side
- **Vulnerability of civilization** — modern military technology proves helpless against a sufficiently advanced adversary
- **Science as both weapon and undoing** — the Martians' advanced technology is ultimately defeated by biology, not human ingenuity
- **Human hubris** — confidence in progress and empire collapses swiftly under the invasion

## Key Takeaways
- The Martians land in cylinders on Horsell Common, near the narrator's home in Woking, Surrey
- Their tripod fighting-machines wield a Heat-Ray and Black Smoke, weapons far beyond contemporary human arms
- The ironclad warship Thunder Child's suicidal ramming of two tripods is the novel's key moment of human resistance
- The Martians are ultimately killed by ordinary Earth bacteria, not by any human weapon, a famously anticlimactic and ironic resolution
- The narrator's entrapment with the curate near the Martian pit lets Wells show the aliens' feeding habits up close
- Red weed, a Martian plant that spreads rapidly over the English countryside, visually marks the invasion's spread
- Comparison point: a sudden, present-day invasion by an external technological power, contrasting with The Time Machine's slow-motion evolutionary decline

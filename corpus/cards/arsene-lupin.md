# The Extraordinary Adventures of Arsène Lupin — Maurice Leblanc

## Summary
The Extraordinary Adventures of Arsène Lupin (1907) collects Maurice Leblanc's earliest stories about the gentleman-thief Arsène Lupin, a master of disguise, safecracking, and audacious theft who taunts police and victims alike with advance warnings and a puckish sense of fair play. Told largely by an admiring narrator, the nine stories, including "The Arrest of Arsène Lupin," "Arsène Lupin in Prison," "The Escape," and "The Queen's Necklace," establish Lupin's recurring rivalry with the dogged police inspector Ganimard and showcase his method of hiding in plain sight, often among the very people hunting him. Leblanc created Lupin partly as a French answer to Sherlock Holmes, and the collection's final story, "Sherlock Holmes Arrives Too Late," directly stages that rivalry by having the English detective summoned only to find Lupin has already outmaneuvered everyone. Wildly popular in France, the Lupin stories founded a long-running series prized for their ironic twists, disguised identities, and the thief's charm as an anti-hero who steals from the rich and the corrupt.

## Plot
Across nine linked stories, Arsène Lupin repeatedly proves himself the most brilliant and elusive criminal in France. In "The Arrest of Arsène Lupin," he is finally caught by Inspector Ganimard aboard an ocean liner, only for the narrator to reveal a final twist about Lupin's true identity among the passengers. Imprisoned, he nonetheless carries out an audacious robbery of the Château de Cahorn by mail in "Arsène Lupin in Prison," mocking the authorities from behind bars, then engineers his own escape from custody in "The Escape." Later stories show him operating through disguise and misdirection: outwitting a fellow traveler in "The Mysterious Traveller," investigating a historic diamond theft tied to his own childhood in "The Queen's Necklace," solving a card-marked mystery in "The Seven of Hearts," infiltrating a fraudulent household built on a rumored fortune in "Madame Imbert's Safe," and orchestrating the theft of a famous jewel in "The Black Pearl." The collection closes with "Sherlock Holmes Arrives Too Late," in which the English detective is summoned to recover stolen property but arrives only after Lupin has already resolved the affair entirely to his own advantage, setting up their rivalry in later books.

## Characters
- **Arsène Lupin** — gentleman-thief and master of disguise, narrates or engineers most of the collection's crimes with wit and flair
- **Inspector Ganimard** — the dogged Sûreté detective who repeatedly pursues Lupin and becomes his semi-respectful recurring rival
- **Sherlock Holmes** — the English detective, summoned in the closing story only to find Lupin has already won
- **The narrator** — an admiring, often unnamed acquaintance of Lupin's who relates several of the exploits at second hand

## Themes
- **The charming criminal** — Lupin is framed as a rogue whose wit and code of honor make his crimes forgivable
- **Disguise and misdirection** — nearly every story turns on Lupin's hidden identity, revealed only in a final twist
- **Rivalry with authority** — recurring bouts with Ganimard and, finally, Holmes structure Lupin's reputation as uncatchable
- **Class and targets of theft** — Lupin typically preys on the wealthy, aristocratic, or fraudulent rather than the poor

## Key Takeaways
- Lupin was conceived partly as a French rival to Sherlock Holmes, culminating in their direct meeting in the collection's final story
- "The Arrest of Arsène Lupin" ends with a twist revealing Lupin's identity among the ship's passengers all along
- Even imprisoned, Lupin manages to rob the Château de Cahorn by mail in "Arsène Lupin in Prison"
- Inspector Ganimard is Lupin's most persistent pursuer across the collection, a rivalry that continues into later Leblanc novels
- "Sherlock Holmes Arrives Too Late" has the famous English detective arrive only after Lupin has already resolved the case himself
- The stories established Lupin as one of French popular fiction's most enduring anti-heroes, still adapted a century later
- Comparison point: a detective-adjacent rogue's-gallery collection that sits naturally beside the Sherlock Holmes stories it was written to rival

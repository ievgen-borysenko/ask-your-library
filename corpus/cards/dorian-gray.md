# The Picture of Dorian Gray — Oscar Wilde

## Summary
The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890, revised 1891) is Oscar Wilde's only novel, a Gothic tale of aestheticism and moral corruption. The beautiful young Dorian Gray, painted by the artist Basil Hallward, is drawn into a cult of pleasure and beauty by the cynical, epigrammatic Lord Henry Wotton, whose philosophy convinces Dorian to wish that his portrait would age and bear the marks of sin in his place while he himself remains eternally youthful. The wish is granted, and as Dorian pursues decades of hedonism, cruelty, and secret vice, including the ruin of the actress Sibyl Vane, his portrait grows increasingly hideous while his own face stays flawless. Hidden away, the portrait becomes both his conscience and his damning record. The novel closes with Dorian, tormented by guilt, stabbing the portrait in an attempt to destroy his past, only to die instantly and grotesquely aged, while the portrait reverts to its original beauty. It stands as a defining work on the corrupting cost of vanity and unchecked pleasure.

## Plot
Basil Hallward paints a portrait of the strikingly beautiful young Dorian Gray, and introduces him to his friend Lord Henry Wotton, whose seductive philosophy of pleasure, beauty, and the primacy of youth deeply influences Dorian. Struck by the fear that his beauty will fade while the portrait remains young, Dorian impulsively wishes the painting would age instead of him, and the wish is inexplicably granted. Dorian falls in love with a young actress, Sibyl Vane, but cruelly rejects her after a poor stage performance; she kills herself, and Dorian notices the first cruel change appear on his hidden portrait. Guided increasingly by Lord Henry's amoral philosophy, Dorian spends the following years pursuing pleasure and secret vice while his public reputation and unchanging youthful face protect him from suspicion, even as rumors of his corrupting influence spread. He murders Basil Hallward after the painter confronts him about his degraded life and the hidden, now-monstrous portrait. Dorian's guilt and paranoia mount, including an encounter with Sibyl Vane's vengeful brother James, who is killed in a hunting accident before he can take revenge. Ultimately unable to bear the portrait's hideous testimony to his corruption, Dorian stabs the canvas in a final attempt to destroy his conscience. His servants find him dead, withered and unrecognizably aged, a knife in his heart, while the portrait on the wall has returned to its original youthful beauty.

## Characters
- **Dorian Gray** — a beautiful young man whose wish for eternal youth transfers his moral corruption onto his portrait
- **Basil Hallward** — the painter of Dorian's portrait, whose devotion to Dorian's beauty leads to his own murder
- **Lord Henry Wotton** — a cynical aristocrat whose hedonistic philosophy corrupts Dorian's worldview
- **Sibyl Vane** — a young actress whom Dorian loves and cruelly discards, leading to her suicide
- **James Vane** — Sibyl's brother, who seeks revenge on Dorian for his sister's death

## Themes
- **Vanity and the cost of eternal youth** — Dorian's unchanging face conceals a soul rotting under the portrait's hidden decay
- **Aestheticism versus morality** — the novel stages Wilde's own tension between "art for art's sake" and moral consequence
- **Corrupting influence** — Lord Henry's epigrams and philosophy actively shape and ruin Dorian's character
- **The double life** — Dorian's respectable public face and hidden vices dramatize Victorian anxieties about hypocrisy
- **The picture as conscience** — the portrait functions as an external, visible record of sin that the body no longer bears

## Key Takeaways
- Dorian's wish that the portrait age instead of him is the novel's central supernatural premise and moral engine
- Sibyl Vane's suicide, after Dorian rejects her for a bad performance, marks the first visible corruption on the hidden portrait
- Lord Henry Wotton's paradox-laden aphorisms, such as praising pleasure over conscience, drive much of the novel's philosophical texture
- Dorian murders Basil Hallward, the man who painted the portrait and first tried to warn him of his corruption
- The portrait, locked away in a shuttered upper room, grows steadily more hideous as a hidden record of Dorian's sins
- Dorian's final act of stabbing the portrait to destroy his past instead kills him and restores the painting's beauty
- Comparison point: a Gothic tale of secret transformation and hidden monstrousness that sits naturally beside Frankenstein and Dracula

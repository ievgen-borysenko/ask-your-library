# The Time Machine — H. G. Wells

## Summary
The Time Machine (1895) is H. G. Wells's first novel and one of the founding texts of science fiction, structured as a frame narrative in which an unnamed guest recounts the tale told by his host, the Time Traveller, at a London dinner party. Having built a machine that carries him forward to the year 802,701 AD, the Time Traveller finds humanity split into two descendant species: the frail, childlike Eloi living an idle surface existence, and the brutish, subterranean Morlocks who maintain the machinery of their world and, it turns out, prey on the Eloi by night. Wells uses this bifurcation to project Victorian class divisions to a horrifying evolutionary extreme, then pushes even further into deep time to show the Earth itself dying under a swollen red sun. The novel remains a touchstone for stories about far-future decline and the fragility of "progress."

## Plot
The Time Traveller demonstrates a miniature time machine to skeptical dinner guests, then, in the next scene, arrives late to the same dinner exhausted and disheveled to tell what happened. He describes travelling to the year 802,701 AD, where he finds the Eloi, small and incurious surface-dwellers who show no industry or ambition, and discovers his time machine has been dragged away and hidden. Exploring, he learns of the Morlocks, pale ape-like creatures who live underground tending old machinery and who emerge at night to prey on the Eloi as food. He rescues an Eloi woman, Weena, from drowning, and she becomes his companion; she dies when Morlocks set fire to the forest during his fight to retrieve the machine. Having recovered it, he travels much further forward, to a nearly lifeless Earth some thirty million years hence, under a vast dim red sun, where he witnesses giant crabs and the final stillness of a dying world before returning to his own time. He tells this story to his disbelieving guests and, taking supplies, departs again on the machine; the frame narrator closes the book still waiting, years later, for his return.

## Characters
- **The Time Traveller** — unnamed inventor-protagonist whose rational, scientific mind confronts a devolved far future
- **Weena** — an Eloi woman the Time Traveller rescues and befriends; dies in a forest fire during his escape from the Morlocks
- **The narrator ("I")** — unnamed frame narrator and dinner guest who records the Time Traveller's account
- **The Eloi** — small, childlike far-future humans descended from the leisured class
- **The Morlocks** — pale, subterranean, light-averse far-future humans descended from the laboring class

## Themes
- **Class division taken to evolutionary extreme** — the Eloi and Morlocks are literalized descendants of leisure and labor
- **Critique of social Darwinism and "progress"** — comfort and idleness are shown producing degeneration, not advancement
- **Entropy and the fate of the universe** — the far-future vision of a dying Earth under a cooling sun dramatizes physical law over human hope
- **Degeneration rather than progress** — the future is depicted as decline, inverting Victorian faith in inevitable improvement

## Key Takeaways
- The year 802,701 AD is the specific far-future date the Time Traveller first visits
- The Morlocks feeding on the Eloi is revealed gradually, turning an idyllic-seeming future sinister
- The final, even further journey to a dying Earth under a red sun extends the story's timescale to tens of millions of years
- Weena's death in the forest fire is the story's emotional low point and cost of the Time Traveller's escape
- The frame narrative device (a story told at a dinner party, then left open-ended) leaves the Time Traveller's ultimate fate unresolved
- Comparison point: this is Wells's far-future vision of gradual social decay, contrasting with The War of the Worlds' sudden, present-day external invasion

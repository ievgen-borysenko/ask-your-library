# Pride and Prejudice — Jane Austen

## Summary
Pride and Prejudice (1813) follows Elizabeth Bennet, the witty second of five daughters in a genteel but financially precarious family at Longbourn, as she navigates courtship, class, and her own misjudgments alongside the wealthy, initially proud Mr. Darcy. The arrival of the amiable Mr. Bingley at nearby Netherfield draws Elizabeth's elder sister Jane into a promising romance, while Elizabeth's early dislike of Darcy, fueled by his aloof manner and the lies of the charming but duplicitous Mr. Wickham, gives the novel its title tension: pride on Darcy's side, prejudice on hers, each mirroring the other's flaw. Austen's precise social comedy, rendered largely through dialogue and free indirect discourse, examines marriage as both an emotional and an economic necessity for women of the period. The novel's slow-burning reversal, culminating in Elizabeth's revised understanding of Darcy's true character, remains one of English literature's most celebrated romantic arcs.

## Plot
Mr. Bingley leases Netherfield Park near the Bennet family's home; his friend Mr. Darcy attends the local ball and offends Elizabeth Bennet by declaring her "not handsome enough to tempt me." Jane Bennet and Bingley grow close, but Darcy and Bingley's sisters intervene to separate them. Mr. Collins, the obsequious clergyman cousin who stands to inherit Longbourn, proposes to Elizabeth and is refused; he marries her friend Charlotte Lucas instead. Visiting Charlotte at Hunsford, Elizabeth encounters Darcy again, nephew of the imperious Lady Catherine de Bourgh; Darcy proposes and is refused, Elizabeth citing his role in separating Jane and Bingley and his mistreatment of Wickham. Darcy's subsequent letter reveals the truth: Wickham had attempted to elope with Darcy's young sister Georgiana, and Darcy's objections to Jane were made in good faith. Visiting Darcy's estate, Pemberley, with her aunt and uncle Gardiner, Elizabeth's opinion of him softens. When Lydia Bennet elopes with Wickham, threatening the family's reputation, Darcy secretly arranges and funds their marriage. Bingley and Jane become engaged, and after Lady Catherine's attempt to intimidate Elizabeth into refusing Darcy backfires, Darcy proposes a second time and is accepted.

## Characters
- **Elizabeth Bennet** — witty, independent second daughter; the novel's moral and perceptive center
- **Mr. Darcy** — wealthy, initially proud owner of Pemberley, who grows more open over the novel
- **Jane Bennet** — the gentle eldest daughter, in love with Bingley
- **Mr. Bingley** — amiable, easily-influenced new neighbor at Netherfield
- **Mr. Collins** — obsequious clergyman cousin and heir to Longbourn through entail
- **Mr. Wickham** — charming militia officer later revealed as a fortune-hunter and would-be seducer
- **Lydia Bennet** — youngest, reckless Bennet daughter who elopes with Wickham
- **Mr. Bennet** — Elizabeth's ironic, emotionally detached father
- **Mrs. Bennet** — anxious mother fixated on marrying off her daughters
- **Charlotte Lucas** — Elizabeth's pragmatic friend, who marries Collins for financial security
- **Lady Catherine de Bourgh** — Darcy's domineering aunt, who tries to prevent his marriage to Elizabeth

## Themes
- **Marriage as economic and social necessity** — the entail on Longbourn and the family's limited fortune shape every courtship
- **First impressions versus true character** — pride and prejudice are mirrored flaws each protagonist must overcome
- **Class and social mobility** — the gap between Darcy's wealth and the Bennets' modest standing drives much of the plot's tension
- **Wit and irony as social critique** — Austen's narration and dialogue expose the absurdities of Regency courtship rituals

## Key Takeaways
- Darcy's first-ball insult, calling Elizabeth "not handsome enough to tempt me," sets up the novel's title tension of pride and prejudice
- Darcy's letter after his first, rejected proposal is the structural hinge that reverses Elizabeth's understanding of him
- Mr. Collins's proposal and subsequent marriage to Charlotte Lucas dramatizes marriage as pragmatic economic security rather than romantic choice
- Lydia's elopement with Wickham threatens to ruin the whole family's reputation until Darcy secretly resolves it
- The Longbourn entail, which bars the Bennet daughters from inheriting, drives the family's urgency around marrying well
- Elizabeth's visit to Pemberley, seeing Darcy's home and hearing his housekeeper's praise of him, is a key turning point in her opinion of him
- Lady Catherine de Bourgh's attempt to browbeat Elizabeth into refusing Darcy inadvertently prompts his second, successful proposal

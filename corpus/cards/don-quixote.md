# Don Quixote — Miguel de Cervantes

## Summary
Don Quixote (Part One, 1605), Miguel de Cervantes's foundational novel, follows an aging La Mancha gentleman who, having read too many chivalric romances, loses his grip on reality and sets out as a self-styled knight-errant, renaming himself Don Quixote, his old horse Rocinante, and an unremarkable local woman his idealized lady, Dulcinea del Toboso. He recruits the practical, proverb-spouting peasant Sancho Panza as his squire with promises of adventure and eventual governorship of an island, and the pair's misadventures across the Spanish countryside, most famously Quixote's attack on windmills he believes are giants, repeatedly expose the gap between his chivalric fantasy and mundane reality. Episodic and richly comic, the novel blends slapstick, embedded tales, and sharp satire of romance literature with a surprisingly affectionate portrait of its deluded hero's idealism. Widely regarded as the first modern novel, it established the enduring comic archetype of the impractical idealist paired with a down-to-earth companion.

## Plot
Alonso Quixano, an elderly, impoverished gentleman obsessed with romances of chivalry, loses his sanity and reinvents himself as the knight-errant Don Quixote de la Mancha, donning old armor, renaming his broken-down horse Rocinante, and declaring a local peasant girl, whom he has never properly met, his beloved lady Dulcinea del Toboso. He sets out alone on a first sally, is comically beaten and humiliated, and returns home, only to set out again after recruiting his neighbor, the simple but shrewd peasant Sancho Panza, as his squire, promising him eventual reward and governorship of an island in exchange for loyal service. Together they undertake a series of misadventures across the Spanish countryside: Quixote famously charges a row of windmills, mistaking them for giants, and is thrown from his horse; he battles flocks of sheep believing them armies, frees a chain of galley slaves who then turn on him, and repeatedly mistakes ordinary inns, barbers, and travelers for castles, enchanters, and knights out of romance. Sancho, torn between skepticism and loyalty, tries to manage his master's delusions while pursuing his own more practical interests. Concerned friends, the village priest and barber, stage schemes to bring the increasingly battered and delusional Quixote home, including burning his library of chivalric romances, and Volume One ends with Quixote returned to his village, his sanity and fate left open for the continuing narrative.

## Characters
- **Don Quixote (Alonso Quixano)** — an idealistic, delusional gentleman who reinvents himself as a knight-errant after reading too many chivalric romances
- **Sancho Panza** — Quixote's practical, proverb-loving peasant squire, drawn by promises of reward and island governorship
- **Dulcinea del Toboso** — an ordinary peasant woman whom Quixote idealizes, unmet, as his chivalric lady
- **Rocinante** — Quixote's aged, broken-down horse, comically elevated to a noble steed in his imagination
- **The Priest and the Barber** — Quixote's concerned village friends who scheme to bring him home and burn his chivalric library

## Themes
- **Illusion versus reality** — Quixote's chivalric fantasy repeatedly collides with the mundane, often painful, truth of his surroundings
- **Satire of chivalric romance** — the novel parodies the popular romance literature that drove Quixote to madness
- **Idealism and its costs** — Quixote's noble intentions consistently result in humiliation, injury, and confusion for himself and others
- **Master and servant as complementary opposites** — Sancho's earthy pragmatism balances and grounds Quixote's lofty delusion
- **The birth of the modern novel** — Cervantes's self-aware, layered narration and psychological depth broke from earlier romance conventions

## Key Takeaways
- Quixote's attack on windmills, believing them giants, is literature's most famous image of noble delusion meeting stubborn reality
- Sancho Panza's promised reward, governorship of an island, becomes a running joke throughout Quixote's recruitment pitch
- Quixote's freeing of the chained galley slaves, who promptly turn on him and his squire, satirizes his blind chivalric idealism
- Dulcinea del Toboso exists only in Quixote's imagination, built from an ordinary peasant woman he has barely met
- Quixote's friends burn his library of chivalric romances in an attempt to cure the obsession driving his madness
- The novel's self-referential narration, including a claimed found-manuscript source, was groundbreaking for its era
- Widely credited as the first modern novel, it established the enduring literary pairing of an idealist and a pragmatic companion

# Celebrated Crimes — Alexandre Dumas

## Summary
Celebrated Crimes (1839-1840) is a collection of true-crime essays by Alexandre Dumas, written early in his career before his famous novels, retelling infamous historical episodes of murder, intrigue, and injustice drawn largely from French, Italian, and European history. Ranging from Renaissance Italy's Borgia family and the tragic patricide case of Beatrice Cenci, to the political assassination of dramatist August von Kotzebue by the student Karl Ludwig Sand, the downfall of Mary Queen of Scots, and the notorious witchcraft trial and execution of the priest Urbain Grandier at Loudun, the essays combine historical research with Dumas's flair for dramatic narrative and vivid character portraiture. Rather than dry chronicle, each piece is shaped like a story, with villains, victims, and moral judgment rendered in Dumas's characteristically vivid prose, prefiguring the historical-adventure techniques he would later deploy in novels like The Three Musketeers. The collection reflects Dumas's lifelong fascination with the theatrical possibilities of real history and remains a notable early example of popular narrative true crime.

## Structure
Celebrated Crimes is organized as a series of self-contained historical essays, each devoted to a single notorious case or family drawn from several centuries of European, chiefly Italian and French, history. Among the episodes are essays on the Borgia family, focusing on Pope Alexander VI and his children Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia's alleged poisonings and political machinations in Renaissance Italy; the Cenci case, recounting the trial and execution of Beatrice Cenci for the murder of her abusive father in sixteenth-century Rome; the assassination of the writer August von Kotzebue by the radical student Karl Ludwig Sand in early nineteenth-century Germany; the downfall and execution of Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots; and the trial and burning of the priest Urbain Grandier, convicted of witchcraft amid the mass demonic possessions at Loudun. Each essay follows a broadly narrative arc, establishing historical context, tracing the build-up to the crime or downfall, and rendering the trial or execution as a dramatic set piece, with Dumas frequently editorializing on injustice, fanaticism, or moral corruption.

## Key Figures
- **Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia** — Renaissance Italy's most notorious political family, their essay recounting alleged poisonings and ruthless ambition under Pope Alexander VI
- **Beatrice Cenci** — a young Roman noblewoman executed for the murder of her abusive father, her case treated as a tragedy of injustice
- **Karl Ludwig Sand** — the German student whose assassination of dramatist Kotzebue is examined as an act of political fanaticism
- **Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots** — the deposed and executed Scottish queen, whose downfall Dumas frames as political tragedy
- **Urbain Grandier** — the priest burned for witchcraft amid the mass possessions at Loudun, his trial presented as a case of religious hysteria and injustice

## Themes
- **Injustice and abuse of power** — several essays, especially the Cenci and Grandier cases, frame their subjects as victims of corrupt or fanatical authority
- **Historical crime as narrative drama** — Dumas applies novelistic pacing and character-building to real historical trials and killings
- **Political intrigue and ambition** — the Borgia and Mary Stuart essays trace how power and ambition drive historical catastrophe
- **Religious fanaticism** — the Grandier case in particular indicts mass hysteria and clerical corruption at Loudun

## Key Takeaways
- The Borgia essay centers on Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia's reputation for poison and political murder under their father Pope Alexander VI
- Beatrice Cenci's case is presented sympathetically, framing her father's murder as a desperate response to his abuse
- Karl Ludwig Sand's assassination of August von Kotzebue is treated as a case study in political fanaticism among radicalized students
- The Urbain Grandier essay recounts the Loudun possessions and the priest's execution for witchcraft amid religious hysteria
- Mary Stuart's essay traces her downfall and execution as a story of political tragedy and courtly betrayal
- The collection predates Dumas's famous novels, showing an early interest in dramatizing real historical intrigue that later shaped The Three Musketeers
- Comparison point: a true-crime historical collection that shares Dumas's taste for court intrigue, betrayal, and dramatic reckoning with his own The Three Musketeers

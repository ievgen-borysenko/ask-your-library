# Gulliver's Travels — Jonathan Swift

## Summary
Gulliver's Travels (1726), Jonathan Swift's satirical prose masterpiece, recounts four voyages of the English ship's surgeon Lemuel Gulliver to fantastical lands, each used to satirize different aspects of human nature, politics, and society. In Lilliput, Gulliver is a giant among six-inch inhabitants embroiled in absurd political and religious disputes; in Brobdingnag, he is a tiny curiosity among giants who force him to confront the pettiness of human institutions when described plainly to a wise king. His third voyage takes him to the floating island of Laputa and related lands of impractical scientists and philosophers absorbed in useless abstraction, and his final voyage brings him among the rational, horse-like Houyhnhnms and the brutish, degraded human-like Yahoos, forcing a bleak reassessment of humanity itself. Framed as a straightforward travel narrative, the book functions throughout as biting political and philosophical satire of Swift's own eighteenth-century England, and remains a foundational text of English satirical fiction.

## Plot
Lemuel Gulliver, an English ship's surgeon, is shipwrecked in Lilliput, where he is captured by six-inch-tall inhabitants and becomes entangled in their absurdly petty political conflicts, including a war with the neighboring island of Blefuscu fought partly over which end of an egg should be cracked. He helps Lilliput militarily but falls from favor and flees to Blefuscu before returning home. His second voyage strands him in Brobdingnag, a land of giants, where he is kept as a curiosity and pet by a farmer's family and later the royal court; his descriptions of European institutions strike the wise Brobdingnagian king as evidence of a "pernicious race of little odious vermin." A third voyage brings him to the flying island of Laputa and its subject land, populated by scientists and philosophers so absorbed in abstract, impractical research that they neglect basic function and comfort, a satire of misapplied science and academia. His final voyage takes him to the land of the Houyhnhnms, intelligent and rational horses who live by reason and virtue, contrasted with the Yahoos, filthy, degraded creatures resembling humans in form but governed entirely by base appetite. Gulliver comes to admire the Houyhnhnms so completely that, on returning to England, he finds ordinary human society repulsive, preferring the company of horses to that of his own family, ending the novel on a note of bitter misanthropy.

## Characters
- **Lemuel Gulliver** — the narrator, an English ship's surgeon whose four voyages expose him to increasingly pointed satires of humanity
- **The Lilliputians** — six-inch inhabitants of Lilliput, whose petty political and religious disputes satirize European court politics
- **The Brobdingnagian King** — a wise giant ruler whose plain-spoken judgment of European society voices Swift's sharpest political critique
- **The Laputans and Academy scientists** — impractical intellectuals absorbed in useless abstraction, satirizing misapplied scientific research
- **The Houyhnhnms** — rational, virtuous horse-like beings representing an idealized standard of reasoned existence
- **The Yahoos** — filthy, degraded humanoid creatures embodying humanity's basest instincts

## Themes
- **Satire of politics and human pettiness** — Lilliput's disputes over trivial matters mock the disproportionate stakes of real political conflict
- **Relativity of perspective** — Gulliver's shifting size between voyages repeatedly reframes what counts as grand or trivial
- **Critique of science and academia** — the Laputan Academy satirizes research disconnected from practical human need
- **Misanthropy and the limits of reason** — the Houyhnhnms and Yahoos force a bleak reassessment of human nature's rational and bestial poles
- **Travel narrative as satirical vehicle** — the book's realistic, matter-of-fact narration heightens the absurdity of what it describes

## Key Takeaways
- The Lilliputian war over which end of an egg to crack satirizes the triviality underlying real religious and political conflict
- Gulliver extinguishes a palace fire in Lilliput by urinating on it, an act that ironically costs him royal favor
- The Brobdingnagian king's verdict that Europeans are "the most pernicious race of little odious vermin" is the novel's sharpest political line
- The Laputan Academy's scientists pursue absurd, impractical projects like extracting sunbeams from cucumbers, satirizing misapplied research
- The Houyhnhnms and Yahoos represent the novel's starkest opposition between idealized reason and degraded animal instinct
- Gulliver's final revulsion at ordinary humans, preferring horses' company after his last voyage, marks the book's bleakest satirical turn
- The novel's four-part structure lets each voyage satirize a different target: politics, perspective, science, and human nature itself

# Treasure Island — Robert Louis Stevenson

## Summary
Treasure Island (1883) is the novel that codified the modern pirate adventure, narrated largely by young Jim Hawkins, who works at his family's inn, the Admiral Benbow. A map found in a dead pirate's sea-chest sends Jim, Squire Trelawney, and Dr. Livesey on a voyage aboard the Hispaniola to find buried treasure, unaware that most of the crew, secretly recruited by the one-legged cook Long John Silver, are former pirates planning mutiny. The book's enduring power comes from Silver, a villain of genuine charm and shifting loyalties who is neither fully trustworthy nor fully evil, and from Jim's own growth from an observant boy into someone capable of decisive, sometimes reckless, action. Its map, stockade siege, and treasure hunt established the genre's stock imagery for the century that followed.

## Plot
Jim Hawkins discovers a treasure map in the belongings of the pirate Billy Bones after his death at the Admiral Benbow Inn. Squire Trelawney and Dr. Livesey fund an expedition aboard the Hispaniola, unwittingly hiring Long John Silver as ship's cook, who staffs the crew with his own former pirate shipmates. Hidden in an apple barrel, Jim overhears Silver's plan for mutiny once the treasure is found. On reaching the island, the mutiny breaks out; Jim goes ashore and meets Ben Gunn, a pirate marooned there years earlier who already knows where the treasure is. Captain Smollett and the loyal party retreat to a stockade and repel a pirate assault. Jim slips away, cuts the Hispaniola loose from the anchorage, and kills the mutinous Israel Hands in a fight aboard ship. Captured by the pirates on his return, Jim is protected from execution by Silver, who has struck a private bargain with Livesey. The pirates, now holding the map, march inland to dig for the treasure only to find the site already emptied by Ben Gunn. In the ensuing fight the remaining pirates flee, and the party sails home with the treasure, Silver slipping away with a portion of the loot during a stop at a Spanish port.

## Characters
- **Jim Hawkins** — young narrator and innkeeper's son whose courage and impulsiveness repeatedly tip the story's balance
- **Long John Silver** — one-legged ship's cook and secret pirate captain, charming, cunning, and morally ambiguous
- **Dr. Livesey** — physician and magistrate, the expedition's steady, rational voice
- **Squire Trelawney** — wealthy, talkative gentleman who finances and nearly sabotages the voyage through indiscretion
- **Captain Smollett** — disciplined, wary ship's captain who distrusts the crew from the start
- **Billy Bones** — old pirate lodging at the Admiral Benbow whose sea-chest holds the map
- **Ben Gunn** — marooned former pirate who found the treasure years earlier and hid it himself
- **Israel Hands** — mutinous coxswain killed by Jim in a shipboard duel
- **Blind Pew** — pirate who delivers the "black spot" death-mark to Billy Bones

## Themes
- **Moral ambiguity** — Silver embodies loyalty and betrayal at once, resisting a simple hero/villain divide
- **Coming of age** — Jim's initiative repeatedly outpaces the adults around him, marking his transition from boy to actor
- **Greed versus loyalty** — the treasure hunt tests every character's true allegiances
- **Adventure and the map-and-treasure trope** — the novel originates much of the genre's now-standard imagery

## Key Takeaways
- The treasure map with its "X marks the spot" and the term "black spot" both originate from this novel's imagery
- Long John Silver's parrot, Captain Flint, and his catchphrase-like squawking became an enduring pirate-fiction motif
- Jim's eavesdropping from the apple barrel is the pivotal moment that exposes the mutiny plot in time
- Ben Gunn, marooned and half-wild, had already dug up and re-hidden the treasure before the Hispaniola even arrives
- Silver escapes justice at the end, slipping away with some of the treasure rather than facing punishment
- Comparison point: a crewed, treasure-driven sea adventure with a morally ambiguous antagonist (Silver), contrasting with Crusoe's solitary, providence-focused survival

# Alice's Adventures in Wonderland — Lewis Carroll

## Summary
Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865) follows a young girl named Alice who falls down a rabbit hole while chasing a White Rabbit and finds herself in a nonsensical dreamworld governed by its own shifting logic. Episodic rather than tightly plotted, the book strings together a series of encounters, size-changing potions and cakes, a grinning Cheshire Cat, an endless Mad Tea Party, and the tyrannical Queen of Hearts, each testing Alice's grip on identity, language, and reason against absurd rules and wordplay. Lewis Carroll, the pen name of mathematician and logician Charles Dodgson, laces the book with parody and logical games that appeal to adult readers even as its dream-logic narrative charms children. The story closes with Alice waking from the entire adventure as a dream, told afterward to her sister, cementing its place as a foundational work of literary nonsense and one of the most quoted and adapted books in English.

## Plot
Alice follows a White Rabbit down a rabbit hole into Wonderland, where she drinks and eats items labelled "Drink Me" and "Eat Me" that repeatedly shrink and grow her, at one point nearly drowning in a pool of her own tears. She attends a chaotic Caucus race, receives advice on controlling her size from a hookah-smoking Caterpillar perched on a mushroom, and meets the perpetually grinning Cheshire Cat, who appears and disappears at will. At the Duchess's house she encounters a baby that turns into a pig, then joins the Mad Hatter and March Hare's endless tea party, stuck perpetually at six o'clock, alongside the sleepy Dormouse. She wanders into the Queen of Hearts's croquet ground, played with flamingos as mallets and hedgehogs as balls, where the Queen constantly orders "Off with her head!" for minor offenses. The Gryphon and the melancholy Mock Turtle relate their absurd school days and perform the Lobster Quadrille. The story culminates in the trial of the Knave of Hearts, accused of stealing the Queen's tarts; as the trial descends into chaos, Alice grows to giant size, denounces the court as "nothing but a pack of cards," and wakes up on the riverbank, the whole adventure revealed as a dream she recounts to her sister.

## Characters
- **Alice** — curious young protagonist navigating Wonderland's dream logic
- **The White Rabbit** — anxious, perpetually late rabbit whose pursuit leads Alice into Wonderland
- **The Cheshire Cat** — a grinning, riddling cat able to disappear, leaving only its smile behind
- **The Mad Hatter** — eccentric host of an endless tea party stuck at six o'clock
- **The March Hare** — the Hatter's equally eccentric tea party companion
- **The Dormouse** — the sleepy third guest at the Mad Tea Party
- **The Queen of Hearts** — tyrannical ruler of Wonderland, quick to order beheadings over trifles
- **The King of Hearts** — the Queen's milder, more reasonable husband
- **The Caterpillar** — a hookah-smoking creature who questions Alice's sense of identity
- **The Mock Turtle and the Gryphon** — melancholic and eccentric pair who recount their absurd schooling to Alice
- **The Duchess** — owner of a chaotic kitchen and a baby who transforms into a pig

## Themes
- **Logic and nonsense** — Wonderland's rules parody formal logic, math, and language while subverting sense at every turn
- **Loss of identity and growing up** — Alice's constant size changes destabilize her sense of self throughout the story
- **Dream logic** — events follow associative, dreamlike causality rather than a conventional plot
- **Satire of Victorian manners and education** — the Mock Turtle's lessons and the tea party's etiquette parody adult social rituals
- **Arbitrary authority** — the Queen of Hearts's constant, groundless death sentences satirize despotic power

## Key Takeaways
- The "Drink Me" bottle and "Eat Me" cake are the story's recurring devices for Alice's dramatic size changes
- The Cheshire Cat's ability to vanish while leaving its grin behind is one of the book's most famous images
- The Mad Tea Party is stuck perpetually at six o'clock, a running joke about Time itself having quarreled with the Hatter
- The Queen of Hearts's catchphrase "Off with her head!" epitomizes the book's satire of arbitrary tyranny
- The Knave of Hearts's trial for stealing tarts is the book's climactic set piece, ending in the court's collapse
- The entire adventure is revealed as Alice's dream, framed by her sister's presence at the start and end
- The book is widely read on two levels: a children's fantasy and an adult game of logical and linguistic parody

# Ivanhoe — Walter Scott

## Summary
Ivanhoe (1819), Walter Scott's influential historical novel, is set in 12th-century England during the aftermath of the Third Crusade and the tension between Saxon and Norman nobility. Wilfred of Ivanhoe, a disinherited Saxon knight loyal to the absent King Richard the Lionheart, returns in disguise to compete at a great tournament, where he wins acclaim before being wounded and nursed by Rebecca, the daughter of the Jewish moneylender Isaac of York. The novel weaves together Ivanhoe's love for the Lady Rowena, his father Cedric's Saxon nationalism, the Knights Templar villain Brian de Bois-Guilbert's obsession with Rebecca, and the machinations of Prince John, who schemes against his brother Richard's throne. Robin Hood and his outlaw band play a supporting role in besieging the castle of the cruel Norman baron Front-de-Boeuf to rescue the captured heroes. The novel closes with Richard's return, Ivanhoe's marriage to Rowena, and Rebecca's quiet, unrequited nobility, and it helped popularize the romanticized medieval England of tournaments, knights, and outlaws still recognizable today.

## Plot
Wilfred of Ivanhoe, a Saxon knight disinherited by his father Cedric for supporting the Norman King Richard and loving the Saxon ward Rowena, returns secretly from the Crusades disguised as a pilgrim. He competes anonymously at a grand tournament at Ashby, defeating several Norman knights including the Templar Brian de Bois-Guilbert, but is seriously wounded in a later contest. He is nursed by Rebecca, daughter of the Jewish moneylender Isaac of York, who has fallen in love with him despite knowing it can never be returned. Prince John, scheming to seize the throne from his crusading brother Richard, and the Norman baron Front-de-Boeuf capture a party including Cedric, Rowena, Isaac, Rebecca, and the wounded Ivanhoe, imprisoning them at Front-de-Boeuf's castle, Torquilstone. Robin Hood and his band of outlaws, aided by a mysterious Black Knight who is secretly King Richard traveling incognito, besiege and burn the castle to free the prisoners. Bois-Guilbert, obsessed with Rebecca, abducts her to a Templar preceptory, where she is condemned as a witch and must be defended by a champion in trial by combat; Ivanhoe arrives just in time to fight for her, and Bois-Guilbert dies of his own inner conflict rather than by Ivanhoe's blow. King Richard reveals himself, reasserts his throne against Prince John's schemes, and reconciles Cedric to his son. The novel ends with Ivanhoe's marriage to Rowena, while Rebecca and Isaac depart England, her quiet nobility left unrewarded by the romantic plot.

## Characters
- **Wilfred of Ivanhoe** — a disinherited Saxon knight loyal to King Richard, torn between Rowena's hand and Rebecca's devotion
- **Rebecca** — Isaac of York's daughter, wise and courageous, who loves Ivanhoe and is later tried as a witch
- **Rowena** — the Saxon noblewoman Ivanhoe ultimately marries, favored by his father Cedric
- **Cedric of Rotherwood** — Ivanhoe's Saxon nationalist father, who disinherits him before eventual reconciliation
- **Brian de Bois-Guilbert** — a Knights Templar warrior obsessed with Rebecca, whose internal conflict leads to his death
- **Prince John** — Richard's scheming younger brother, seeking to usurp the English throne in his absence
- **King Richard the Lionheart** — England's absent king, who travels incognito as the "Black Knight" to reclaim his throne
- **Robin Hood** — the outlaw leader (called Locksley) whose band aids the siege of Torquilstone
- **Isaac of York** — Rebecca's father, a Jewish moneylender subjected to the era's antisemitic prejudice

## Themes
- **Saxon versus Norman tension** — the novel dramatizes lingering ethnic and political conflict a century after the Norman Conquest
- **Chivalry and its limits** — tournament pageantry and knightly honor are tested against real cruelty and prejudice
- **Religious and ethnic prejudice** — Rebecca and Isaac's treatment exposes medieval and, implicitly, contemporary antisemitism
- **Romanticized medievalism** — Scott's vivid tournaments, castles, and outlaws helped shape the popular image of medieval England
- **Loyalty and legitimacy** — the novel contrasts Prince John's usurping schemes with Richard's rightful, if absent, kingship

## Key Takeaways
- Ivanhoe's disguised victory at the Ashby tournament, unmasking himself only after winning, is the novel's most iconic scene
- The Black Knight who aids Robin Hood's band at Torquilstone is revealed to be King Richard traveling incognito
- Rebecca's trial for witchcraft, resolved by Ivanhoe's trial-by-combat victory, is the novel's central climax
- Robin Hood, here called Locksley, appears as a supporting ally rather than the story's central figure
- The siege and burning of Front-de-Boeuf's castle at Torquilstone is one of Scott's most celebrated action sequences
- Rebecca's unrewarded nobility and departure from England, despite her virtue, has long drawn critical attention as the novel's unresolved injustice
- The novel helped popularize enduring images of medieval chivalry, tournaments, and Robin Hood still current in modern culture

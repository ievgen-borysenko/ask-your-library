# Jane Eyre — Charlotte Brontë

## Summary
Jane Eyre (1847) is Charlotte Brontë's first-person bildungsroman following its orphaned title character from a loveless childhood at Gateshead Hall, through the harsh charity school Lowood, to her position as governess at Thornfield Hall, where she falls in love with her enigmatic employer, Edward Rochester. Jane's fierce insistence on self-respect and moral independence, unusual for a Victorian heroine, drives the novel's central crisis: on their wedding day she discovers Rochester is already married to Bertha Mason, a madwoman he keeps hidden in Thornfield's attic. Jane flees, nearly perishes on the moors, and is taken in by the Rivers siblings, who turn out to be her cousins and make her a modest heiress. Refusing a loveless marriage proposal from the pious St. John Rivers, Jane returns to a blinded, widowed Rochester after a fire destroys Thornfield and kills Bertha, and the novel ends with their marriage as equals. It remains a foundational work of psychological realism and feminist-inflected Victorian fiction.

## Plot
Orphaned Jane Eyre endures a cruel childhood with her aunt, Mrs. Reed, at Gateshead Hall before being sent to Lowood, a charity school run with harsh discipline by the hypocritical Mr. Brocklehurst, where her friend Helen Burns dies of illness. After years as a pupil and teacher there, Jane takes a post as governess at Thornfield Hall, tutoring the ward Adèle under the byronic, secretive master Edward Rochester. Jane and Rochester fall in love despite his volatile temperament and mysterious household disturbances, including a fire in his room and an attack on a guest, Mr. Mason. On their wedding day, Mason interrupts to reveal Rochester is already married to his sister, Bertha Mason, a violently insane woman Rochester has kept confined in Thornfield's attic for years. Jane flees, nearly dying of exposure on the moors, and is rescued by the clergyman St. John Rivers and his sisters, who turn out to be her cousins; she also inherits a fortune from an uncle. St. John pressures Jane to marry him and join his missionary work in India, but she refuses, hearing what she believes is Rochester's voice calling her. Returning to Thornfield, she finds it burned down, Bertha dead in the fire, and Rochester blinded and maimed while trying to save the household. Jane marries him as his equal, and the novel closes with their reconciled, loving marriage.

## Characters
- **Jane Eyre** — orphaned, principled narrator who insists on emotional and moral independence throughout her hardships
- **Edward Rochester** — Thornfield's brooding master, secretly married, who becomes Jane's love and eventual husband
- **Bertha Mason** — Rochester's first wife, confined in the attic due to violent insanity, whose existence upends Jane's wedding
- **Mrs. Reed** — Jane's cold aunt at Gateshead, whose cruelty shapes Jane's early sense of injustice
- **Mr. Brocklehurst** — Lowood's hypocritical headmaster, preaching austerity while living in comfort
- **Helen Burns** — Jane's devout, saintly friend at Lowood, who dies of consumption
- **St. John Rivers** — Jane's cousin and a zealous clergyman who proposes a loveless, duty-bound marriage
- **Adèle Varens** — Rochester's young ward, Jane's pupil at Thornfield

## Themes
- **Moral and emotional independence** — Jane repeatedly refuses relationships that would compromise her self-respect, even Rochester's initial bigamous proposal
- **Social class and gender constraint** — Jane's position as a poor governess shapes every choice available to her
- **Religion and hypocrisy versus true faith** — Brocklehurst's false piety contrasts with Helen Burns's genuine, quiet devotion
- **The madwoman in the attic** — Bertha Mason's confinement raises enduring questions about Victorian treatment of women and mental illness
- **Equality in marriage** — the novel's ending insists on partnership between Jane and Rochester rather than dependence

## Key Takeaways
- Jane's declaration "Reader, I married him" is one of English literature's most famous closing lines
- Bertha Mason's confinement in Thornfield's attic gave rise to the enduring critical phrase "the madwoman in the attic"
- Jane flees her own wedding upon learning Rochester's first wife is still alive, refusing to become his mistress
- The fire that blinds Rochester and kills Bertha allows Jane to return to him as an independent, propertied equal
- St. John Rivers's proposal, offered as duty rather than love, is the novel's clearest contrast to genuine affection
- Jane's unexpected inheritance from her uncle in Madeira gives her the financial independence to choose freely
- Comparison point: a governess's marriage-plot novel of moral autonomy that sits beside Wuthering Heights and Pride and Prejudice in the Victorian courtship tradition

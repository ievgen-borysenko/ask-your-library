# Wuthering Heights — Emily Brontë

## Summary
Wuthering Heights (1847), Emily Brontë's only novel, is a dark, structurally complex tale of passion and revenge set on the Yorkshire moors, narrated largely through the outsider Mr. Lockwood and the housekeeper Nelly Dean's retrospective account. It follows Heathcliff, a foundling raised alongside Catherine Earnshaw at Wuthering Heights, whose intense, destructive bond with Catherine is thwarted when she marries the gentler Edgar Linton of neighboring Thrushcross Grange for social position. Heathcliff's grief and fury after Catherine's death drive him to spend the rest of his life exacting revenge on both families, degrading Catherine's brother Hindley's son Hareton and manipulating his own son's marriage to Catherine and Edgar's daughter, young Cathy. Spanning two generations across a bleak, storm-swept setting, the novel defies Victorian conventions of moral resolution, ending only when the surviving younger Cathy and Hareton form a gentler bond that hints at healing, while Heathcliff dies still haunted by Catherine's ghost.

## Plot
Mr. Lockwood, a new tenant at Thrushcross Grange, visits his reclusive landlord Heathcliff at the nearby farmhouse Wuthering Heights and, after a disturbing night in Catherine's old room, asks the housekeeper Nelly Dean to explain the household's history. Nelly recounts how Heathcliff, an orphan brought home by Mr. Earnshaw, grew up alongside Earnshaw's children Hindley and Catherine, forming an intense bond with Catherine while Hindley, jealous, degrades Heathcliff to a servant after their father's death. Catherine, drawn to the refined Edgar Linton of Thrushcross Grange, marries him for status despite loving Heathcliff, who overhears only part of her explanation and leaves in despair. He returns wealthy years later and sets about ruining both households: he marries Edgar's sister Isabella to torment her, gains control of Wuthering Heights by exploiting Hindley's gambling and drinking, and reduces Hindley's son Hareton to an uneducated laborer. Catherine dies giving birth to a daughter, also named Catherine (Cathy), and Heathcliff is consumed by grief, believing himself haunted by her ghost. Years later, Heathcliff forces a marriage between his own sickly son Linton and young Cathy to seize the Linton estate as well. After Linton's death and Heathcliff's continued cruelty, young Cathy and Hareton, initially antagonistic, gradually form an affectionate bond. Heathcliff, worn down by his obsession with the dead Catherine, dies, and the novel ends with the prospect of Cathy and Hareton's marriage restoring peace to both houses.

## Characters
- **Heathcliff** — orphaned foundling whose thwarted love for Catherine drives a lifelong campaign of revenge on both families
- **Catherine Earnshaw** — passionate, torn between her wild bond with Heathcliff and her marriage to Edgar Linton for status
- **Edgar Linton** — Catherine's gentle, conventional husband, master of Thrushcross Grange
- **Hindley Earnshaw** — Catherine's brother, whose cruelty to Heathcliff in youth sets the revenge plot in motion
- **Isabella Linton** — Edgar's sister, married by Heathcliff purely as an instrument of his revenge
- **Hareton Earnshaw** — Hindley's son, degraded by Heathcliff, later redeemed through young Cathy's affection
- **Cathy Linton** — Catherine and Edgar's daughter, forced into marriage with Linton Heathcliff before finding happiness with Hareton
- **Nelly Dean** — the housekeeper whose retrospective narration frames most of the novel
- **Mr. Lockwood** — the outsider tenant whose curiosity prompts Nelly's telling of the story

## Themes
- **Destructive passion** — Heathcliff and Catherine's bond is depicted as overwhelming rather than redemptive, fueling ruin rather than happiness
- **Revenge across generations** — Heathcliff's campaign against the Earnshaws and Lintons extends into the children's own marriages
- **Nature versus civilization** — the wild moor setting of Wuthering Heights contrasts with the refined domesticity of Thrushcross Grange
- **Social class and marriage** — Catherine's choice of Edgar over Heathcliff is explicitly driven by status and property
- **Haunting and the supernatural** — Catherine's ghost and Heathcliff's belief in her presence blur the line between grief and the uncanny

## Key Takeaways
- The novel's frame narrative, Lockwood's visit and Nelly Dean's retelling, distances the reader from events by two removes
- Catherine's famous declaration "I am Heathcliff" captures the novel's vision of identity fused rather than merely in love
- Heathcliff's revenge plot spans two generations, degrading Hareton and forcing a marriage between Cathy and his own son
- The two houses, wild Wuthering Heights and refined Thrushcross Grange, function as symbolic opposites throughout
- Heathcliff dies believing himself reunited with Catherine's ghost, having refused food in his final days
- The younger generation's ending, Cathy and Hareton's growing bond, offers the novel's only note of reconciliation
- Comparison point: a dark, passion-driven marriage-plot novel that sits beside Jane Eyre and Pride and Prejudice as a study of Victorian courtship and social constraint, though far bleaker in tone

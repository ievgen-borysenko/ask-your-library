# Frankenstein — Mary Shelley

## Summary
Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818) is framed as letters from Arctic explorer Robert Walton to his sister, into which he embeds the confession of Victor Frankenstein, a scientist he rescues from the ice. Victor recounts how, as a student at Ingolstadt, he discovered the secret of animating dead matter and assembled a creature from scavenged body parts, only to be overwhelmed with horror at what he had made and abandon it. The unnamed Creature, initially gentle and eager to belong, is driven to violence by relentless rejection, and the novel becomes a chase across Europe and into the Arctic as creator and creation destroy each other's lives. Written by the then-19-year-old Shelley, it is a foundational work of science fiction that fuses Gothic horror with Romantic questions about ambition, responsibility, and what makes a being monstrous.

## Plot
Victor Frankenstein, obsessed since childhood with alchemy and later modern chemistry, builds a human-like being from assembled corpse parts and animates it in his Ingolstadt lodgings, then flees in horror at its appearance, abandoning it. The Creature, alone and reviled by everyone he meets, secretly observes the De Lacey family in a cottage for months, teaching himself language and reading (including Paradise Lost), before being violently driven off when he finally reveals himself. Enraged, he strangles Victor's young brother William; the family servant Justine Moritz is wrongly convicted and executed for the murder. The Creature confronts Victor on the Mer de Glace above Chamonix and demands Victor create a female companion for him. Victor agrees, begins the work on a remote Orkney island, then destroys the half-finished female out of fear of a monstrous race; the Creature vows revenge, killing Victor's friend Henry Clerval and, on Victor's wedding night, his bride Elizabeth Lavenza. Victor's father dies of grief, and Victor pursues the Creature into the Arctic, where Walton finds him near death. Victor dies aboard Walton's ship; the Creature appears, grieves over his creator's body, and vows to end his own life on a funeral pyre before vanishing across the ice.

## Characters
- **Victor Frankenstein** — obsessive scientist whose ambition to conquer death leads him to abandon and then be destroyed by his own creation
- **The Creature** — unnamed being assembled by Victor; articulate and initially benevolent, turns vengeful after sustained rejection
- **Robert Walton** — frame narrator, an Arctic explorer whose own consuming ambition mirrors Victor's
- **Elizabeth Lavenza** — Victor's adopted sister and bride, murdered by the Creature on their wedding night
- **Henry Clerval** — Victor's devoted childhood friend, murdered by the Creature
- **William Frankenstein** — Victor's young brother, the Creature's first victim
- **Justine Moritz** — family servant wrongly executed for William's murder
- **Alphonse Frankenstein** — Victor's father, dies of grief after the family's losses

## Themes
- **Unchecked ambition / "playing God"** — Victor's pursuit of forbidden knowledge brings catastrophe rather than glory
- **Nature versus nurture** — the Creature is born capable of gentleness and learning, and is made violent by rejection, not innate evil
- **Responsibility of creator to creation** — Victor's central sin is abandonment, not the act of creation itself
- **Isolation and alienation** — both Victor and the Creature are cut off from human community, with destructive results
- **The sublime in nature** — Alpine and Arctic landscapes mirror the characters' emotional extremity

## Key Takeaways
- The Creature is never given a name in the text; "Frankenstein" properly refers to the creator, a common misconception worth noting
- The novel is structured as nested narratives: Walton's letters frame Victor's story, which frames the Creature's own account
- The Creature's self-education by observing the De Lacey family and reading Paradise Lost is key to his sense of injustice
- Victor's destruction of the half-made female companion is the turning point that seals both their fates
- The famous line "I will be with you on your wedding night" is the Creature's threat before killing Elizabeth
- Comparison point: the Creature is a made, initially innocent victim turned violent by rejection, unlike Dracula's predatory, deliberately evil monster
- The novel closes on ambiguity in the Arctic wastes, with neither creator nor creation granted resolution or redemption

# Robinson Crusoe — Daniel Defoe

## Summary
Robinson Crusoe (1719) is often called the first English novel, presented as the autobiography of a York merchant's son who defies his father's wish for a quiet "middle station" life and goes to sea. After early misadventures, including capture by Sallee pirates and a stint as a Brazilian plantation owner, Crusoe is shipwrecked alone near the mouth of the Orinoco River and spends 28 years on an uninhabited island. The novel traces his practical mastery of the island (shelter, farming, livestock) alongside a parallel inward journey from irreligious rashness to providential faith, recorded in the journal he keeps. His rescue of a native man he names Friday, and their master-servant relationship, gives the book its most debated and influential episode. It endures as a foundational text of individualism, colonial ideology, and the survival-narrative genre.

## Plot
Crusoe runs away to sea against his father's advice, is enslaved at Sallee, escapes with the boy Xury, and becomes a successful plantation owner in Brazil. Embarking on a slave-trading voyage, he is shipwrecked as the sole survivor and washes ashore on an island near the Orinoco. He salvages tools and supplies from the wreck, builds a fortified shelter, domesticates goats, grows barley and rice, and keeps a journal weighing his misfortunes against his blessings. After a severe illness he undergoes a religious conversion, turning to the Bible for comfort. Around fifteen years in, he discovers a single footprint in the sand and later witnesses cannibal feasts on the shore, living in fear before rescuing a captive he names Friday, whom he teaches English and Christianity. Later he rescues a Spaniard and Friday's father from cannibals. Near the end of his stay, an English ship arrives whose crew has mutinied against its captain; Crusoe helps the captain retake the vessel and is given passage home after 28 years, 2 months, and 19 days on the island. He later revisits the island as its acknowledged "governor."

## Characters
- **Robinson Crusoe** — narrator and castaway; matures from a rash, disobedient youth into a disciplined, providence-trusting survivor
- **Friday** — native man Crusoe saves from cannibals; becomes his loyal companion and servant, taught English and Christianity
- **Crusoe's father** — urges him to accept a comfortable "middle station" life, which Crusoe rejects
- **Xury** — the boy who helps Crusoe escape slavery at Sallee early in the story
- **The Spaniard** — a shipwrecked captive Crusoe rescues from the cannibals alongside Friday's father
- **The English captain** — his ship is seized by mutineers; Crusoe helps him retake it, securing his own passage home

## Themes
- **Providence and religious conversion** — Crusoe reads his ordeal as divine punishment and then guidance, tracked through his journal
- **Self-reliance and labor** — survival is rebuilt piece by piece through inventive, methodical work
- **Colonialism and mastery** — Crusoe's rule over the island and over Friday reflects period assumptions about civilization and race
- **Solitude** — decades of isolation reshape his relationship to society, faith, and himself

## Key Takeaways
- Crusoe keeps a running ledger of "evil and good" to rationally counterbalance his despair, an early example of journaling as self-management
- The single footprint in the sand, discovered after roughly fifteen years alone, is the novel's most famous moment of dread
- His fortified shelter, goat-herding, and grain-growing form a detailed manual of self-sufficient survival
- Friday's rescue and the naming of him after the day of the week originates the "man Friday" trope for a devoted assistant
- Crusoe's religious conversion during illness reframes the whole ordeal as a story of spiritual salvage, not just physical survival
- Total time on the island (28 years, 2 months, 19 days) is one of the book's most quoted concrete details
- Useful contrast point: solitary, self-made survival (Crusoe) versus crewed, treasure-driven adventure (Treasure Island)

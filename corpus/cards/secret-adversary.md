# The Secret Adversary — Agatha Christie

## Summary
The Secret Adversary (1922) is Agatha Christie's second published novel and the first to feature Tommy Beresford and Prudence "Tuppence" Cowley, two broke, adventure-hungry friends from the Great War who form "The Young Adventurers, Ltd." to take on any job, however risky. Their first assignment pulls them into a hunt for Jane Finn, an American woman who vanished carrying a secret draft treaty salvaged from the sinking of the Lusitania, and into the orbit of a shadowy criminal mastermind known only as Mr. Brown. Backed unofficially by the secret intelligence officer Mr. Carter and the wealthy American Julius Hersheimmer, Tommy and Tuppence's amateur sleuthing, disguise, and narrow escapes gradually expose a conspiracy reaching into respectable British society. The novel launched Christie's long-running, lighter-toned Tommy and Tuppence series, distinct from her puzzle-driven Poirot novels for its thriller pacing and the couple's romantic partnership, which culminates here in their engagement.

## Plot
Tommy Beresford and Tuppence Cowley, old friends short on money after the war, agree over tea to advertise themselves as "Young Adventurers" willing to do almost anything for pay. Tuppence's spontaneous use of the name "Jane Finn" during a chance encounter with the sinister Mr. Whittington draws them into a real mystery: the actual Jane Finn vanished years earlier while carrying a secret document salvaged from the torpedoed Lusitania, a paper whose exposure could destabilize the government. The pair is soon recruited properly by the intelligence official Mr. Carter and aided by Julius Hersheimmer, Jane Finn's wealthy American cousin, as they search London and the countryside for Jane and the missing papers. Tuppence, working undercover as a lady's maid, is kidnapped, while Tommy tracks a criminal network led by the mysterious Mr. Brown, whose true identity is disguised among trusted figures. Amid red herrings pointing at Julius and others, the amnesiac young woman Annette, who once saved Tuppence, is revealed to be Jane Finn herself. In the climax, the respected lawyer and politician Sir James Peel Edgerton is unmasked as Mr. Brown, the criminal mastermind operating from within respectable society. The recovered treaty is secured, and Tommy and Tuppence, having proven themselves, become engaged.

## Characters
- **Tommy Beresford** — a resourceful, steady young war veteran, one half of the amateur detective duo
- **Tuppence Cowley** — quick-witted and impulsive, drives much of the plot's risk-taking before marrying Tommy at the novel's end
- **Jane Finn (Annette)** — the missing American woman carrying the secret treaty, found suffering amnesia under a false identity
- **Mr. Julius Hersheimmer** — Jane Finn's wealthy, brash American cousin who funds and joins the search
- **Mr. Carter** — the unofficial head of British intelligence who recruits Tommy and Tuppence
- **Sir James Peel Edgerton** — respected barrister and politician ultimately unmasked as the criminal mastermind Mr. Brown
- **Mr. Whittington** — a sinister agent whose chance encounter with Tuppence launches the entire adventure

## Themes
- **Amateur versus professional detection** — Tommy and Tuppence's youthful daring succeeds where official channels stall
- **Hidden identity in respectable society** — Mr. Brown's unmasking as a trusted public figure is the novel's central shock
- **Post-war restlessness** — the pair's search for adventure and income reflects a generation displaced by the First World War
- **Partnership and romance** — Tommy and Tuppence's growing trust and eventual engagement runs alongside the thriller plot

## Key Takeaways
- Tuppence's improvised alias "Jane Finn," spoken on a whim, accidentally triggers the entire conspiracy plot
- The secret document at the center of the plot was salvaged from the sinking of the Lusitania
- The villain Mr. Brown is revealed as Sir James Peel Edgerton, a pillar of the legal and political establishment
- Jane Finn spends much of the novel in plain sight under the alias Annette, suffering from amnesia
- The novel founded Christie's Tommy and Tuppence series, lighter and more thriller-paced than her Poirot mysteries
- Julius Hersheimmer, the wealthy American cousin, is deliberately written as a suspicious red herring throughout
- Comparison point: an amateur-detective thriller of disguise and conspiracy that sits naturally beside Arsène Lupin's gentleman-adventurer stories

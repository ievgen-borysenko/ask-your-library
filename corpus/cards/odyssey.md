# The Odyssey — Homer

## Summary
The Odyssey is an ancient Greek epic poem in 24 books attributed to Homer, recounting the ten-year journey of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, as he tries to return home after the fall of Troy while his wife Penelope fends off a house full of suitors and his son Telemachus comes of age in his absence. Structurally the poem interlaces Telemachus's own search for news of his father with Odysseus's famous first-person account of his wanderings, the Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Underworld, before he is finally returned home by the seafaring Phaeacians. The poem's second half turns from fantastical adventure to a tense homecoming plot, as the disguised Odysseus tests loyalties, reunites with his family, and violently reclaims his household from the suitors. Central to the epic are the values of cunning intelligence (metis), hospitality (xenia), and the long-delayed satisfaction of nostos, homecoming, making it a foundational text of Western narrative and of the hero defined as much by wit as by strength.

## Structure
The poem's 24 books fall into recognizable movements. The opening "Telemachy" (Books 1-4) follows Telemachus traveling to Pylos and Sparta seeking news of his missing father. Books 5-8 introduce Odysseus himself, held captive for years by the nymph Calypso on Ogygia before being shipwrecked among the Phaeacians, where King Alcinous and princess Nausicaa receive him. Books 9-12 contain Odysseus's own first-person narration of his earlier adventures: the Cicones, the Lotus-Eaters, the Cyclops Polyphemus, Aeolus and the bag of winds, the Laestrygonians, the sorceress Circe, a journey to consult the dead in the Underworld, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the crew's fatal slaughter of the Cattle of the Sun. Books 13-24 cover his return to Ithaca disguised as a beggar, his reunions with Telemachus, the swineherd Eumaeus, and his old nurse Eurycleia, the contest of the bow that only he can string, and the climactic slaughter of Penelope's suitors, followed by his reunion with Penelope and his father Laertes.

## Characters
- **Odysseus** — cunning king of Ithaca ("man of many wiles"), struggling for ten years to return home from Troy
- **Penelope** — Odysseus's faithful wife, who delays remarriage by unraveling her weaving each night
- **Telemachus** — Odysseus's son, who comes of age searching for news of his father
- **Athena** — goddess who repeatedly protects and guides both Odysseus and Telemachus
- **Poseidon** — sea god and Odysseus's divine antagonist, angered by the blinding of his son Polyphemus
- **Polyphemus** — the Cyclops, son of Poseidon, blinded by Odysseus using the "Nobody" trick
- **Circe** — sorceress who turns Odysseus's men into swine before becoming his ally and host for a year
- **Calypso** — nymph who holds Odysseus captive on her island of Ogygia for years
- **The Suitors** — led by Antinous and Eurymachus, they court Penelope while consuming Odysseus's estate
- **Eumaeus** — the loyal swineherd who shelters and aids the disguised Odysseus
- **Nausicaa** — the Phaeacian princess who discovers the shipwrecked Odysseus

## Themes
- **Nostos (homecoming)** — the long-deferred return home is the poem's central driving desire
- **Hospitality (xenia) and its abuse** — the suitors' violation of guest-host norms at Odysseus's own house is a central moral wrong
- **Cunning intelligence (metis) versus brute strength** — Odysseus repeatedly triumphs through trickery rather than force alone
- **Fate and divine intervention** — gods actively shape mortal outcomes, with Athena aiding and Poseidon obstructing Odysseus
- **Identity, disguise, and recognition** — Odysseus's return unfolds through a series of tests and recognitions (his scar, the marriage bed, the bow)

## Key Takeaways
- Odysseus blinds the Cyclops Polyphemus and escapes by calling himself "Nobody," delaying pursuit when Polyphemus cries for help
- Circe turns Odysseus's crew into swine before becoming his host and advisor for a year on her island
- The Sirens' song and the strait between Scylla and Charybdis are the epic's most famous navigational perils
- Odysseus's old nurse Eurycleia recognizes him by a childhood scar on his leg while washing his feet
- The contest of the bow, stringing it and shooting an arrow through a row of axe heads, is the trial only Odysseus can pass, triggering the suitors' slaughter
- Penelope tests Odysseus's identity at the very end by referring to their marriage bed's unique, immovable construction
- Comparison point: a crewed, divinely-influenced homecoming epic of cunning and endurance, useful alongside Robinson Crusoe's solitary survival or Treasure Island's crewed adventure for contrasting ancient and modern castaway/voyage narratives

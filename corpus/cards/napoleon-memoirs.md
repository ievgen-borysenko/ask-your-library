# Memoirs of Napoleon Bonaparte — Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne

## Summary
Memoirs of Napoleon Bonaparte, compiled by Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne, offers a firsthand, insider account of Napoleon's life and career by the man who was his close schoolmate at the military academy of Brienne and later served for years as his private secretary during the Consulate. Bourrienne's memoirs trace Napoleon from his Corsican youth and school days through his rise via the Italian and Egyptian campaigns, the coup that made him First Consul, and into the early Empire, offering personal anecdotes, private conversations, and observations of Napoleon's temperament, habits, and working methods unavailable in official histories. Bourrienne, dismissed from Napoleon's service amid allegations of financial impropriety and later serving the restored Bourbon monarchy, writes with a notably critical, sometimes score-settling edge toward his former employer, making the memoirs valuable as counterpoint to more hagiographic accounts. Long popular and controversial for their mixture of genuine intimacy and disputed accuracy, the memoirs remain a widely cited primary-adjacent source for Napoleon's personal character and early career.

## Structure
Bourrienne's memoirs proceed roughly chronologically, opening with his own boyhood friendship with Napoleon at the military school of Brienne, then following Napoleon's early military career, his rise to prominence during the Italian campaign and the Egyptian expedition, his seizure of power in the coup of 18 Brumaire, and his years as First Consul and early Emperor, during which Bourrienne served as his private secretary with close daily access. The account is organized around Bourrienne's own personal proximity to events, blending narrative history with personal anecdote, verbatim recollections of conversations, and character sketches of Napoleon's household, ministers, and family. Later sections address Bourrienne's fall from favor and dismissal from Napoleon's service, after which his account grows noticeably more critical and less admiring, reflecting both genuine grievance and his later political alignment with the restored monarchy.

## Key Figures
- **Napoleon Bonaparte** — the central subject, portrayed through Bourrienne's close personal access from schoolboy years through the Consulate and Empire
- **Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne** — the memoirist himself, Napoleon's childhood friend and private secretary, later dismissed amid financial scandal
- **Josephine Bonaparte** — Napoleon's first wife, described through Bourrienne's observations of the imperial household
- **Members of Napoleon's family and ministers** — figures in Napoleon's inner political and domestic circle depicted through Bourrienne's insider vantage point

## Themes
- **Intimacy versus official history** — Bourrienne's personal access offers detail and anecdote unavailable in formal historical accounts
- **Rise to power** — the memoirs trace Napoleon's ascent from obscure Corsican cadet to First Consul and Emperor
- **Disillusionment and score-settling** — Bourrienne's later, more critical tone reflects his personal fall from Napoleon's favor
- **Contested reliability** — the memoirs have long been debated for accuracy, partly ghostwritten and shaped by Bourrienne's later grievances

## Key Takeaways
- Bourrienne knew Napoleon personally from their shared schooldays at the military academy of Brienne
- He served as Napoleon's private secretary during the Consulate, giving him direct daily access to Napoleon's working habits
- The memoirs are notably more critical of Napoleon than many contemporary accounts, reflecting Bourrienne's later dismissal amid financial scandal
- Bourrienne later served the restored Bourbon monarchy, a political shift that colors the memoirs' retrospective judgments
- The work is valued as an intimate, anecdote-rich counterpoint to official or hagiographic histories of Napoleon
- Historians have long debated the memoirs' reliability, given later editorial involvement and Bourrienne's personal grievances against his former employer
- The account spans Napoleon's Corsican youth, Italian and Egyptian campaigns, the 18 Brumaire coup, and the early Empire

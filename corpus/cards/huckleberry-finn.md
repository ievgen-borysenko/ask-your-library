# Adventures of Huckleberry Finn — Mark Twain

## Summary
Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) is Mark Twain's first-person sequel to The Adventures of Tom Sawyer, narrated by the uneducated but sharply observant Huck Finn as he flees his abusive, alcoholic father and rafts down the Mississippi River with Jim, a slave escaping to freedom. Their journey, interrupted by shore adventures involving con men known as the Duke and the King, feuding families, and various scams, becomes a moral education for Huck, who must repeatedly choose between the racist values he was raised with and his growing loyalty to Jim as a fellow human being. The novel's climax turns on Huck's decision to help free Jim rather than turn him in, famously resolving, despite his conscience telling him it is wrong by the era's standards, to "go to hell" rather than betray his friend. Its vernacular narration, satire of Southern antebellum society, and unflinching treatment of slavery and racism have made it one of the most celebrated, and most contested, novels in American literature.

## Plot
Huck Finn, having received a share of treasure at the end of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer, is being civilized by the Widow Douglas when his abusive father, Pap, reappears and kidnaps him, holding him captive in a cabin. Huck fakes his own death to escape and flees down the Mississippi, soon meeting Jim, Miss Watson's slave, who has run away after overhearing he is about to be sold. The two raft down the river together, forming a deep bond despite Huck's ingrained racist upbringing, and encounter a series of episodes on shore: a deadly feud between the Grangerford and Shepherdson families, and two con men, the self-styled Duke and King, who join the raft and perpetrate a string of frauds, including impersonating relatives of a dead man to steal an inheritance. Huck grows increasingly torn about helping Jim escape slavery, at one point nearly turning him in before deciding, in a pivotal moral crisis, to tear up a letter exposing Jim's whereabouts and declaring he would rather "go to hell" than betray him. The Duke and King eventually sell Jim, leading Huck and a returning Tom Sawyer to stage an elaborate, absurdly theatrical rescue at the Phelps farm. It is ultimately revealed that Jim had already been freed by Miss Watson's will before her death, and the novel closes with Huck resolving to "light out for the Territory" rather than be civilized again.

## Characters
- **Huck Finn** — the narrator, a resourceful, uneducated boy whose moral growth centers on his loyalty to Jim
- **Jim** — Miss Watson's enslaved man, fleeing toward freedom, whose humanity and dignity anchor the novel's moral core
- **Pap Finn** — Huck's abusive, alcoholic father, whose reappearance drives Huck to flee
- **Tom Sawyer** — Huck's friend, whose elaborate, book-derived schemes complicate Jim's rescue late in the novel
- **The Duke and the King** — two con men who join the raft and perpetrate frauds on riverside towns
- **Widow Douglas and Miss Watson** — sisters attempting to "civilize" Huck; Miss Watson is Jim's legal owner

## Themes
- **Slavery and moral conscience** — Huck's internal conflict over helping Jim escape dramatizes the era's moral hypocrisy
- **Freedom versus civilization** — the raft on the river represents freedom, contrasted with the constraints of shore society
- **Satire of Southern society** — feuds, con artists, and mob behavior expose the violence and gullibility of antebellum communities
- **Friendship across racial lines** — Huck and Jim's bond challenges the racist assumptions Huck was raised with
- **Vernacular voice and realism** — Twain's use of regional dialect was groundbreaking for American literary narration

## Key Takeaways
- Huck's decision to tear up his letter exposing Jim and declare he'll "go to hell" is the novel's central moral turning point
- The raft journey down the Mississippi structures the novel's episodic shore adventures and its central friendship
- The Duke and King's frauds, including a fake inheritance scheme, satirize gullibility and greed in river towns
- The Grangerford-Shepherdson feud depicts senseless, deadly violence inherited unquestioningly across generations
- Tom Sawyer's elaborately theatrical, unnecessarily complicated rescue plan for Jim satirizes romantic adventure fiction itself
- It is revealed late that Jim had already been legally freed, recasting the risks taken throughout the rescue
- Ernest Hemingway's claim that all modern American literature stems from this book reflects its lasting influence on American prose style

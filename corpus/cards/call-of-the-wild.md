# The Call of the Wild — Jack London

## Summary
The Call of the Wild (1903) follows Buck, a large, domesticated dog stolen from a comfortable California home and sold into the brutal world of Klondike Gold Rush sled-dog teams, as he sheds civilization's conditioning and reawakens ancestral, wild instincts. Told from Buck's perspective though not in his own words, the novel traces his passage through a succession of owners, from the relatively fair French-Canadian mail carriers Francois and Perrault, to the cruel and incompetent trio Hal, Charles, and Mercedes, to John Thornton, whose genuine care gives Buck his last real bond to human affection. Each stage strips away another layer of domestication under what the novel calls "the law of club and fang," until Buck's growing responsiveness to the wolves' call outside the firelight culminates, after Thornton's death, in his full return to the wild. It stands as a foundational work of American naturalist fiction and a classic adventure of the frozen North.

## Plot
Buck, a St. Bernard-Shepherd mix living an easy life on Judge Miller's California estate, is stolen by a gardener's assistant and sold into the Yukon sled-dog trade during the Klondike Gold Rush. Under Francois and Perrault, fair but hard mail carriers, he learns the harsh survival code of the sled-dog team, eventually killing the lead dog Spitz in a fight for dominance and taking his place. Sold on, he passes through several owners before falling into the hands of Hal, Charles, and Mercedes, inexperienced and cruel newcomers who overload the sled, starve the team, and drive the dogs toward collapse; Buck is saved from death just before they perish by breaking through river ice. John Thornton, who rescued him, becomes Buck's beloved final owner, and their bond grows through shared hardship, including Buck's famous feat of pulling a heavily loaded sled to win a bet for Thornton. As their time together continues, Buck increasingly wanders into the forest, drawn by the distant howling of wolves, forming a tentative bond with a wild wolf and killing a moose during one extended absence. Returning from one such foray, Buck finds Thornton and his companions killed by Yeehat natives; he kills several of the attackers in retribution, then finally and fully answers the call of the wild, joining a wolf pack and becoming, in local legend, a fearsome "Ghost Dog" of the Yukon wilderness.

## Characters
- **Buck** — the protagonist dog, transformed from a pampered California pet into a wild pack leader
- **John Thornton** — Buck's beloved final owner, who rescues him and treats him with genuine affection
- **Francois and Perrault** — French-Canadian mail carriers, Buck's first sled-dog masters, fair though demanding
- **Spitz** — the sled team's original lead dog and Buck's rival, killed by Buck in a fight for leadership
- **Hal, Charles, and Mercedes** — inexperienced, cruel owners who overwork and starve the team, ultimately dying from their own incompetence
- **Judge Miller** — Buck's original, comfortable owner in California before he is stolen

## Themes
- **Nature versus civilization** — Buck's arc reverses domestication, returning him step by step to primal instinct
- **Survival of the fittest** — the "law of club and fang" governs every relationship among the sled dogs and their masters
- **Atavism** — Buck's growing responsiveness to ancestral, wolf-like instincts drives the novel's central transformation
- **Loyalty and love as counterweight to brutality** — Buck's bond with Thornton is the one force that delays his full return to the wild

## Key Takeaways
- Buck's killing of Spitz to become lead dog is the novel's first major demonstration of "the law of club and fang"
- Hal, Charles, and Mercedes's incompetent, cruel mismanagement of the team leads directly to their own deaths on thin ice
- Buck's feat of single-handedly pulling a thousand-pound sled to win Thornton's bet showcases his restored physical power
- The death of John Thornton at the hands of Yeehat natives is the final event severing Buck's last tie to human society
- Buck's transformation ends with him joining a wolf pack, becoming a local legend known as the "Ghost Dog"
- The novel is narrated from close to Buck's point of view without giving him human speech, a distinctive naturalist technique
- Comparison point: a natural-world adventure of survival and instinct, useful alongside human castaway narratives like Robinson Crusoe for contrasting animal versus human responses to a hostile wilderness

# Moby Dick — Herman Melville

## Summary
Moby-Dick (1851) is narrated by Ishmael, a sailor who signs onto the whaling ship Pequod out of Nantucket and finds its voyage commandeered by Captain Ahab's monomaniacal quest for revenge against Moby Dick, the white sperm whale that took his leg. Interweaving a fairly straightforward revenge narrative with lengthy digressive chapters on cetology, whaling technique, and philosophical meditation, Melville builds a novel that is as much an encyclopedic meditation on knowledge, obsession, and the unknowable as it is an adventure story. Ahab's fixation, opposed but never checked by his rational first mate Starbuck, drives the ship and crew toward destruction across a three-day final chase. Largely unappreciated in its own time, the novel is now regarded as a peak of American literature, its opening line "Call me Ishmael" and its white whale among the most recognizable images in the language.

## Plot
Ishmael travels to Nantucket, meets the Polynesian harpooner Queequeg at the Spouter-Inn, and the two sign onto the whaler Pequod. Captain Ahab, largely absent at first, eventually reveals to the crew his true purpose: hunting down Moby Dick, the white whale that maimed him, nailing a gold doubloon to the mast as a reward for whoever first sights it. The voyage crosses oceans, punctuated by numerous "gams," meetings with other whaling ships, several of which carry news or warnings about Moby Dick, including the Rachel, searching for a captain's son lost to the whale. First mate Starbuck, devout and pragmatic, repeatedly but unsuccessfully opposes Ahab's obsession, while the mysterious harpooner Fedallah prophesies the manner of Ahab's death. Numerous chapters detail whale anatomy, classification, and the practical work of whaling (the chase, the kill, the trying-out of blubber) alongside the narrative. In the climactic three-day chase, Moby Dick destroys Ahab's whaleboats one by one; on the third day the whale rams and sinks the Pequod itself, and Ahab, caught in his own harpoon line, is dragged under and killed. Only Ishmael survives, floating on Queequeg's coffin until he is rescued by the Rachel.

## Characters
- **Ishmael** — philosophical narrator and sole survivor of the Pequod's voyage
- **Captain Ahab** — the Pequod's captain, consumed by his quest for revenge on Moby Dick
- **Moby Dick** — the elusive, destructive white sperm whale that maimed Ahab
- **Queequeg** — a harpooner from the South Seas and Ishmael's close friend
- **Starbuck** — the rational, religious first mate who opposes but cannot stop Ahab
- **Stubb** — the Pequod's easygoing second mate
- **Flask** — the Pequod's third mate
- **Fedallah** — Ahab's mysterious Parsee harpooner, who prophesies the manner of his death
- **Pip** — a young cabin boy driven to madness after being left adrift at sea

## Themes
- **Obsession and monomania** — Ahab's fixation on the whale consumes and ultimately destroys him and his crew
- **Man versus nature and the unknowable** — Moby Dick resists full comprehension, embodying nature's indifference to human will
- **Fate versus free will** — the crew's entanglement in Ahab's quest raises questions of complicity and predestination
- **Knowledge versus the sublime** — the novel's exhaustive cetological digressions contrast with the whale's ultimate mystery, epitomized by the chapter on "the whiteness of the whale"

## Key Takeaways
- The novel's opening line, "Call me Ishmael," is among the most famous in American literature
- Ahab nails a gold doubloon to the mast as a reward for the crew member who first spots Moby Dick
- Melville intersperses the narrative with detailed, encyclopedic chapters on whale biology and the whaling industry
- The ship Rachel, searching for a lost boy, both foreshadows the ending and ultimately rescues Ishmael
- Fedallah prophesies the specific and strange circumstances of Ahab's death before it occurs
- The final three-day chase ends with the whale sinking the Pequod itself, not just destroying the whaleboats
- Ishmael survives by floating on Queequeg's coffin, which had been built and then repurposed as a life buoy

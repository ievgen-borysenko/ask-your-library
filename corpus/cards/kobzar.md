# The Kobzar of the Ukraine — Taras Shevchenko

## Summary
The Kobzar of the Ukraine collects English verse translations of selected poems by Taras Shevchenko, Ukraine's foundational national poet, drawn from his landmark collection Kobzar, first published in 1840 and expanded throughout his life. Writing in vernacular Ukrainian at a time when the language was marginalized under the Russian Empire, Shevchenko fused folk-ballad forms with searing political and social critique, addressing serfdom, national identity, and historical memory of the Cossack past. The selection gathers narrative poems such as "Naimechka; or The Servant," the tragedy of an unmarried mother who hides her identity to stay near her son; "The Monk," reflecting on faith and moral struggle; "The Cossacks," evoking Ukraine's martial and historical heritage; and "Hamaleia," celebrating a Cossack hero's exploits, alongside other lyric and narrative pieces. Shevchenko's own life as a former serf, later exiled by the Russian authorities for his nationalist and anti-imperial writing, deeply informs the collection's recurring themes of suffering, injustice, and love of homeland, and the Kobzar remains the central text of Ukrainian national literary and cultural identity.

## Structure
This English-language selection draws from Shevchenko's Kobzar, presenting a sequence of individually titled narrative and lyric poems rather than a single continuous work, reflecting the collection's original structure as an accumulating body of ballads, elegies, and historical poems composed over Shevchenko's life. The included poems range from intimate narrative tragedies centered on individual women and families, such as "Naimechka; or The Servant," to poems of religious and moral reflection like "The Monk," to poems that turn outward to Ukraine's collective history and Cossack heritage, such as "The Cossacks" and "Hamaleia." Across the selection, folk-song rhythms and forms drawn from Ukrainian oral tradition sit alongside more formally ambitious narrative verse, and recurring imagery, the steppe, the Dnieper river, the Cossack past, and the suffering of ordinary people under serfdom, links the individually titled poems into a unified national and moral vision.

## Key Poems
- **"Naimechka; or The Servant"** — a narrative poem about Hanna, an unmarried mother who leaves her infant son Mark with a childless couple, returns to them as a servant to raise him, and reveals that she is his mother only on her deathbed
- **"The Monk"** — a reflective poem exploring religious devotion and inner moral struggle
- **"The Cossacks"** — a poem evoking Ukraine's martial history and the legacy of Cossack resistance and identity
- **"Hamaleia"** — a narrative poem celebrating the exploits of a Cossack hero, drawing on historical and legendary material
- **Other lyric and narrative selections** — shorter poems in the collection extend the volume's recurring concerns with serfdom, homeland, and injustice

## Themes
- **National identity and homeland** — Shevchenko's poems consistently center Ukraine's language, landscape, and history as sources of collective identity
- **Serfdom and social injustice** — poems like "Naimechka" dramatize the suffering of ordinary people, especially women, under an oppressive social order
- **Cossack historical memory** — poems such as "The Cossacks" and "Hamaleia" draw on Ukraine's martial past as a source of national pride and resistance
- **Faith and moral struggle** — "The Monk" reflects Shevchenko's engagement with religious and ethical questions alongside his political concerns
- **Folk tradition and vernacular voice** — Shevchenko's use of Ukrainian folk-ballad forms elevated the vernacular language into serious literary art

## Key Takeaways
- Shevchenko wrote in vernacular Ukrainian at a time when the language was suppressed and marginalized under the Russian Empire
- "Naimechka; or The Servant" is the volume's central narrative tragedy: a mother's lifelong self-denial ends with a deathbed confession to her son
- This English selection (A. J. Hunter's translation) does NOT include "Kateryna"; questions about it cannot be answered from this library
- Shevchenko was born a serf himself before being freed, and his poetry repeatedly draws on firsthand knowledge of serfdom's injustice
- He was later exiled by Russian imperial authorities for his nationalist and anti-imperial writing, a biographical fact that shadows the collection
- Poems like "The Cossacks" and "Hamaleia" draw on Ukraine's Cossack history as a wellspring of national identity and resistance
- The Kobzar collection, expanded throughout Shevchenko's life, remains the foundational text of modern Ukrainian literature
- Shevchenko fused folk-ballad forms drawn from Ukrainian oral tradition with sophisticated narrative and lyric technique
